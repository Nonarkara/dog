# The History of the Digital Pet
### From a turtle on a television screen to a living Thai Bangkaew

*This is the research section: where Expo comes from, what each generation of
digital pet contributed, and which of those ideas this app actually keeps.*

---

## 1. Before the egg: the pet on your desktop (1985–1995)

The first virtual pets were not toys. They were programs that lived on a
desktop and refused to leave.

The earliest widely cited is **Neko** (1992, Macintosh) — a small cat that
chased the mouse cursor around the screen. It was not a pet you raised; it was
a pet you *tolerated*, and the joke was that it would keep drawing attention
to itself whether you wanted it or not. The term "virtual pet" did not exist
yet, so it was called a *desktop pet*.

**PF Magic** turned the idea into a business. Their 1995 release *Dogz: Your
Computer Pet* was the first widely popular virtual pet, followed by *Catz* in
1996 and then *Oddballz*, which substituted "a cross between a walrus and a
rabbit" for the animals. The games ran on 2D hardware with a technique the
company called Ballz, which faked 3D convincingly on machines that could not
afford it.

Two decisions in *Dogz* and *Catz* still define the genre:

1. **The pet acts on its own.** It wanders, it gets hungry, it does not wait
   for you to open a menu. It is an autonomous character, not a progress bar.
2. **It does not punish you for doing something wrong.** PF Magic's stated
   design goal was to avoid penalising the user, and to put the emphasis on
   *user story telling* — you were the owner, and the owner was narrating.

That second decision is the one the genre threw away in 1997, and then spent
the next thirty years trying to get back.

## 2. The egg (1995–1996)

### The origin

**Akihiro Yokoi** was the president of WiZ, a 42-person creative firm that
designed toy concepts and licensed them to larger manufacturers. The idea
arrived in the way ideas usually do: he saw a television commercial in which a
boy was not allowed to bring his pet turtle on holiday.

Yokoi owned a dog, three cats, two parrots and several beetles. He identified
with the boy. If you cannot always bring a living pet with you, he reasoned,
why not bring a virtual one?

The pitch to Bandai in **1995 was rejected**. So he went and did the market
research: in Shibuya he watched how girls actually used handheld electronics,
and concluded that the device had to be *kawaii* — that the existing handheld
game market was aimed at young boys, and a device that could reach young
women would have to be something else entirely.

With **Aki Maita** of Bandai's marketing team, he built it. The first concept
was a wristwatch; the plastic cost made it a keychain instead. The name is
*tamago* (egg) + *uotchi* (watch). In October 1996, **200 prototypes** went
out to high school girls in Shibuya for a consumer test.

**23 November 1996.** Tamagotchi went on sale in Japan. It cost $15–18 in the
United States, which arrived on 28 May 1997, and it sold **ten million units
in under a year**. At its peak in North America, fifteen units a minute were
going out the door. By 2017 the total was **82 million** across more than
fifty distinct models.

The hardware was deliberately, almost aggressively, modest: a black-and-white
LCD of **16 × 32 dots**, three buttons, one battery. There was no colour
because colour was not affordable at the size and price. That constraint
became the style — the *heta-uma* (変態) "weird-creature" movement of the
mid-'90s was exactly the right visual language for what the display could
manage, and Yokoi had gone looking for illustrators who could draw in it
specifically.

In 1997, Yokoi and Maita received the **Ig Nobel Prize** for "diverting
millions of person-hours of work into the husbandry of virtual pets".

## 3. The burden was the point

This is the part that matters for Expo, so it is worth being precise.

The popular retelling is that Tamagotchi was a fad, a gimmick, a toy with a
tiny screen and a lot of beeping. The accurate retelling is that it was a
genuinely cruel machine that happened to be fun, and that the cruelty was
designed on purpose.

Yokoi's argument was that **pets are only cute about a third of the time**,
and that the rest — the feeding, the mess, the sickness, the fact that they
need you at three in the morning — is what makes the relationship real. As he
put it to the *New York Times* in 1997:

> "Pets are only cute 20 to 30 percent of the time, and the rest is a lot of
> trouble, a lot of work. I wanted to incorporate this kind of idea into a
> toy, for pets these days are only considered cute. But I think that you also
> start to love them when you take care of them."

So the design worked backwards from the burden:

- **The pet demanded things in real time.** Six beeps meant it needed
  attention *now*, not when you got round to it.
- **The pet slept at night, and you had to cooperate.** When the Tamagotchi
  went to sleep its owner had to turn the light off, or the pet became
  restless. The toy physically required the human to stop interacting with it.
  This is the single most important mechanic in the lineage and the one most
  often forgotten.
- **The pet could die.** Neglect it and it sickened, then died. In Japan the
  death screen was a **ghost (お化け, obake) hovering beside a gravestone**;
  in the American P1 it was a three-eyed angel; in the P2, a UFO. The ghost
  became a word in Japanese for a neglected pet's spirit, and the distress was
  widely reported: children crying in classrooms, and a psychologist telling
  the *New York Times* that "the toy creates a real sense of loss and a
  mourning process".
- **The pet's adulthood was a verdict on you.** A well-mannered adult
  Tamagotchi meant you had parented it well. The character you got at the end
  was the record of how you had treated the one you had.

And then, when parents complained — loudly, in the press, about children
becoming distracted and distressed — Bandai **added a pause button** to the
second generation. The genre's central tension was negotiated away within a
year.

The cultural reading was inevitable and accurate: the Tamagotchi was an
anticipatory notification system. It wanted your attention on a schedule, and
it was happy to be a substitute for a relationship. Every phone notification
that has ever interrupted a person mid-sentence is a descendant of that beep.

## 4. The fork: raising versus killing (1997)

**Bandai Digimon** arrived in 1997 and split the genre down the middle.
Tamagotchi asked you to *raise* a creature; Digimon asked you to *fight* one.
One produced a nurturing loop, the other a collection-and-combat loop, and
the second turned out to be the more durable of the two — it became a global
franchise, an anime, a card game, and eventually *Pokémon*.

For the purposes of this app the interesting part is what Tamagotchi did
*not* become. It never became an RPG, it never became a collection, and it
never became a platform. It stayed a small animal on a small screen that
wanted something from you.

## 5. The revival and the long tail (2004–2025)

Bandai brought Tamagotchi back in **March 2004** with the *Tamagotchi
Connection*, a new generation with an **IrDA infrared port** — two devices
could meet, play a game, exchange a present, and eventually produce a child
that had to be raised by both owners. The genre's biggest idea after the
original was simply *other people*.

Then the long tail:

| Year | Model | What it added |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2004 | Tamagotchi Connection | IrDA; meeting other people's pets |
| 2008 | Tamagotchi Plus Color | The first colour screen; a new graphic language by JINCO |
| 2010 | Tamagotchi nano, Tama-Go | Physical collectible figures you plug in |
| 2012 | Tamagotchi P's | Touchscreen |
| 2014 | TAMAGOTCHI 4U | G3 sensor; games played by tilting the device |
| 2016 | Tamagotchi m!x | Music and animation |
| 2019 | Tamagotchi ON | Colour, backlit, global debut |
| 2021 | Tamagotchi Pix | **A camera** — you take AR pictures of your pet |
| 2021 | Tamagotchi Smart | Wristwatch form factor, voice recognition, step counter |
| 2023 | Tamagotchi Uni | **Wi-Fi** and the "Tamaverse"; a new personality mechanic |
| 2024 | Tamagotchi Connection (re-release) | The 2004 original, twenty years on |
| 2025 | Tamagotchi Paradise | A zoom dial that moves between four scales of a cell |

Between 2022 and 2023 global sales doubled.

Read that table as a history of *attachment surfaces*. The creature never got
much more complicated. The **device** did: colour, then touch, then tilt, then
a camera, then a wrist, then the network. Every step was about making the pet
more present in the reader's actual day.

The Camera in the Pix and the step counter in the Smart are the interesting
ones. Both point the pet at the **real world** — at your face, at your walk.
The 2021 devices are the moment the digital pet stopped being a separate
artefact and started being a lens onto your own life.

## 6. The dog: Nintendogs (2005)

The single most important game in the lineage for this app is not a Tamagotchi
at all.

**Nintendogs** shipped on the Nintendo DS on **21 April 2005**. It is a
puppy simulator, and it changed the genre in three specific ways:

1. **It was about a dog.** Not a blob, not a cat, not an alien. You chose a
   breed from a kennel, took the puppy home, and named it — **by speaking
   the name into the microphone**. The dog learned to answer to your actual
   voice.
2. **It had real training.** Tricks, obedience competitions, walks, and a
   relationship that responded to how you treated it. The psychology was
   simplified but present: the dog learned what paid.
3. **It could never die.** This is the crucial departure. Nintendogs refused
   the Tamagotchi bargain of punish-the-neglectful-owner. The dog would get
   sad, dirty and neglected, but it would not perish. Nintendo removed the
   cruelty and kept the attachment.

Nintendogs sold enormously — by 2015 it was the **second-best-selling game on
the Nintendo DS**, behind only New Super Mario Bros — and it built an
audience no one had aimed at: young women and girls who had largely been
outside the "games are for boys" marketing that dominated the era. It is
still the best argument that the right virtual pet is a *specific* animal with
a *specific* breed, living with a specific person.

The other half of the lineage is the **Petz** franchise: *Dogz* (1995) and
*Catz* (1996) from PF Magic, acquired by Mindscape in 1998 and by Ubisoft in
2002, still shipping in 2006 on the GBA, DS and Wii. Same premise, bigger
world, more breeds.

## 7. What the genre settled on, and what Expo keeps

Thirty years of virtual pets converged on a short list of mechanics that
actually matter. Here is that list, and what this app does with each one.

| The lineage's lesson | Where it comes from | What Expo does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Real time is the whole point.** The pet drifts whether or not you are looking. | Tamagotchi's beeps | Needs decay runs on one model, applied on load and every minute. Happiness is computed from the other five numbers, never stored twice. |
| **The pet sleeps, and you have to let it.** | The light-off rule | A sleep state with night hours, slow deep breathing, a curled pose, and closed eyes. |
| **Waking it has a price, and the price compounds.** | Not in Tamagotchi — this is Expo's addition | Every wake-up raises restlessness. A dog woken three times in one night will not settle easily again. **The Bed control refuses to put him down and says why.** |
| **The pet's character is a record of how you treated it.** | Tamagotchi's adult stages | A five-trait personality (boldness, sociability, food drive, play drive, sensitivity) fed by an observation log you write. The temperament is shown as a phrase, not a score: *"a dog who notices everything"*. |
| **The person who trains is part of the model.** | Nintendogs; operant conditioning | The Coach measures your **marker timing in milliseconds**, names your approximation step, and tells you when to move from continuous rewards to every-third to unpredictable schedules. |
| **A dog does not die of neglect.** | Nintendogs | Expo's dog never dies. He becomes harder to settle. |
| **Discipline is a button that means something.** | Tamagotchi's scolding | A real rep is distinguished from a rehearsal. Only the real animal moves the strength. |
| **Other people's pets.** | Tamagotchi Connection, IrDA | The Commons. Import a friend's exported dog JSON and their dog appears in the park as a visitor. |
| **The pet should be on the screen you are already on.** | Tamagotchi as a keychain | **One screen.** The document does not scroll. Everything is on a fixed frame: a header band, five regions, and a command bar that never leaves the bottom. |
| **The pet should look at you.** | The Pix's camera | A 3D avatar that reads the dog's own state — breathing rate, tail speed, ear carriage, head tilt — and a model of the real animal if one is attached to the record. |

Two of these deserve naming as **the** design of this app.

**The burden.** Tamagotchi's genius was that it was not always pleasant. The
beeps were an imposition. Expo inherits that: the dog needs things, the
things cost something, and you can be neglecting him. This is the difference
between a virtual pet and a virtual dashboard, and it is the difference
between a toy that is *cute* and one that is *yours*.

**One screen.** The Tamagotchi had one screen because it had a 16×32 display,
not because of a principle — but the constraint produced the behaviour. You
could not scroll to find out what your pet needed. You had to look. A phone
with a feed can scroll; a Tamagotchi cannot. Expo is built on the same
refusal: the document does not scroll, because a page that scrolls forever is a
page whose contents were never decided. The spatial gate fails the build if
the page scrolls down by even one pixel.

## 8. Where the frontier moved in 2026

The last piece of the history is the newest, and it is the one that made
Expo's avatar possible.

For most of the genre's life, the pet was a **sprite**: pixels hand-drawn for
a 32-column display. The alternative was a **model**: a 3D asset, made by
hand or by scanning, at an absurd cost relative to a Tamagotchi's bill of
materials.

Three things changed:

**Image-to-3D became downloadable and good.** Microsoft's **TRELLIS.2**
(December 2025, MIT licence, 4B parameters, native PBR, GLB export) is the
best open model you can run yourself. Tencent ARC's **Pixal3D** (May 2026,
MIT) rebuilds image-to-3D on a TRELLIS.2 backbone with pixel-aligned
projection and a C++/GGML port that runs on Apple Silicon. **Hunyuan3D** still
leads on texture and PBR — under a community licence that excludes the EU,
UK and South Korea. **SF3D** remains the fastest draft.

**The pipeline got wrapped.** **AssetFurnace** (Apache-2.0) is the whole
chain in one place, and the part that matters here: prompt or picture →
textured, **rigged**, animated character, locally on an M-series Mac, with
auto-rig and 21 move clips.

**Photogrammetry became cheap and Apple-native.** **COLMAP 4.x** (BSD) ships
Apple Silicon builds and is the standard first step of every real capture.
**3D Gaussian Splatting** tools — Nerfstudio, Brush, Spiral Studio,
LichtFeld — train a faithful scene from a phone walkaround. And `capture-splat`
does the whole thing from an iPhone on macOS.

The consequence for a digital pet is specific and worth stating plainly:

> Before 2026, a virtual pet could be *any* animal. After 2026, a virtual pet
> can be **your** animal.

A Flux-generated studio reference sheet of a real dog, run through an
image-to-3D pipeline and auto-rigged, gives you a rigged, textured, animated
GLB of a dog that looks like your dog. That is what the "Photo to 3D avatar"
region in this app's record is *for*. It is still a reference — Canine OS runs
no reconstruction itself — but the pipeline it documents is now one command
away, and the output drops straight into the stage.

## 9. Why Expo exists

The genre's history is a long argument about two things that are usually
treated as opposites and are not.

**Attachment requires friction.** The most-loved virtual pets are the ones
that ask for something. A pet that cannot be neglected is a screensaver. A
pet that can be neglected has to be *taken care of*, and taking care of
something is the entire mechanism by which people come to love it.

**But the burden has to be honest.** Tamagotchi's cruelty produced genuine
grief, and Bandai answered with a pause button. Nintendogs refused the cruelty
entirely. The synthesis, thirty years late, is what this app attempts: **a
dog who is never punished with death, but who is harder to reach the longer
you keep him awake.**

And the animal itself should not be a blob. It should be a Thai Bangkaew with
a wolf-grey agouti coat, a charcoal mask, a white blaze, an enormous cream
mane, amber eyes, and a plumed tail carried over the back — because the whole
point of a digital pet is that it is *someone's* animal, and the difference
between a generic blob and your dog is the entire emotional payload.
